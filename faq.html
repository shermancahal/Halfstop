Finding a place

Where the map opens

On a first visit the map asks your browser where you are and opens there. How far it zooms in depends on how sure the answer is: a phone outdoors gets a close view, while a browser guessing from your internet connection gets a wider one rather than a street corner that may be nowhere near you.

Say no and the map simply opens where it was going to anyway. Nothing else changes, and the locate button in the corner of the map still works if you change your mind.

It only does this when nothing else in the link says where to look. A shared view, a shared pin, or a reload of a map you had already moved all open where they point.

Searching

The search box above the map takes a place name and finds towns, campgrounds, trailheads and the like, preferring places near the view you are looking at, because half the creeks in the country share a name.

It searches your own saved work first and shows it at the top, marked as yours: waypoints, tracks and whole folders, by name and by note. That part needs no signal, so it still answers with the phone in aeroplane mode.

Typing a coordinate

The search box also takes a coordinate, in any of the three forms the details panel prints, so anything you read off this app can be pasted straight back into it:

  • 35.960000, -84.280000 — decimal degrees
  • 35°57.600'N 84°16.800'W — degrees and decimal minutes, what most handheld GPS units show
  • 35°57'36.0"N 84°16'48.0"W — degrees, minutes and seconds, as printed on paper maps

The punctuation is taken loosely. Degree signs are optional, a comma and a space work the same, and N, S, E and W can sit on either side of their number or be left out in favour of a minus sign.

Latitude comes first unless the first number is too large to be one, in which case it is read as a longitude and the result says so. The result always shows the coordinate written back out in full, so you can see what it read before you go there.

This is arithmetic, not a lookup, so it works with no signal.

Planning a Shoot

Sunrises and sunsets

Select a pin, or press anywhere on the map, and choose Details. The Photography section lists sunrise and sunset times with their angles, plus golden hour and blue hour times and durations.

Buttons there open light phases, the moon, the Milky Way — where the night is scored out of moonlight and cloud cover — the aurora, eclipse timing, and fog likelihood and type. A last button, Draw, puts the night on the map itself: the bearings where the sun and moon rise and set, where each of them is at this moment, and the Milky Way band and its core.

All of it is worked out for the coordinates of that pin rather than the nearest city, and it is arithmetic rather than a lookup. There is no request behind it, so it answers the same on a ridge with no bars as it does at the kitchen table.

Moon, Milky Way, aurora, eclipse and fog

Moon. Rise and set, the phase, and the percentage lit. The phase is named the way an almanac names it — new, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous, full, waning gibbous, last quarter, waning crescent — and the percentage is how much of the disc is lit, which is a different number from where the moon is in its cycle. Waxing or waning is the useful half of it: it tells you whether tomorrow night will be brighter than tonight or darker, which is what decides whether to go now or wait a week.

Milky Way. Whether the galactic core is up tonight, how high it climbs, the window while the sky is properly dark, and the best nights over the coming weeks. The night is also scored out of two things that can ruin it — how much moon is up during that window, and the cloud cover forecast across it.

Aurora. The chance at your latitude, sourced from NOAA, along with the Kp index. Both feeds come from NOAA’s Space Weather Prediction Center: the chance is read out of the OVATION model, which publishes a whole-globe grid of aurora probability, and the Kp index comes from the planetary K-index feed beside it. Kp is a 0–9 scale of how disturbed the Earth’s magnetic field is, and it matters because it decides how far south the aurora reaches: 4 is unsettled and worth a look north from the northern states, 5 is the storm line, and 7 and above is the rare kind that is visible well south of it. Worth knowing what the model is answering: OVATION says where the aurora is, not whether you will see it. Cloud, the moon and whatever is standing on your northern horizon all still apply.

Eclipse. The next lunar eclipse, its contact times, and whether the moon clears your horizon for them. A lunar eclipse is one event the entire night side of the planet sees at the same moment, so it resolves to arithmetic. Where a solar eclipse falls is a fussier calculation, and Halfstop does not attempt it.

Fog. An hour-by-hour likelihood, with the kind named beside it. Ground fog forms in place: the ground radiates its heat away under a clear sky, cools the air touching it past its dewpoint, and a light stir spreads that cooling up through a layer deep enough to see. It needs night, a clear sky, and wind that is light but not absent — dead calm makes dew instead — and it is what fills a hollow or a river bottom by dawn and burns off an hour or two after the sun reaches it. Drifting fog is carried in: moist air moving over colder ground, cooling from below as it travels. It needs more wind than ground fog tolerates, ignores the sky and the hour, comes off water and off spring snowpack, and leaves when the wind changes rather than on any schedule. Either can be freezing fog when it happens at or below freezing, which is called out separately because it says nothing about how the fog formed and everything about what it will do to a road and a tripod.

Two kinds are missing on purpose. Upslope fog turns on the wind’s direction against the shape of the ground, and steam fog turns on the temperature of the water it comes off; neither of those is in the forecast feed, and guessing at them would be inventing a forecast rather than reading one. A lake shore or a steep hollow will fog when this says it will not.

Which way to face

The last tab draws the plan on the ground: bearings from the pin to the sun, to the moon and to the galactic core, with the Milky Way band itself as the wide curve. The slider moves time, and the lines swing with it.

That is the point of drawing them. A table can tell you the sun comes up on a bearing of 71°; only the map tells you the ridge is in the way.

Photographs on a waypoint

Select a saved waypoint, choose Details, and the Photos section has Add photos and Take one. On a phone, Take one opens the camera.

Pictures are resized on the way in: 1024 pixels on the long edge, under 100 KB each. That is full width on a phone screen, and small enough that a folder with a hundred of them still opens quickly. A photograph already smaller than that is kept exactly as it arrived.

They stay on the device they were added to. Nothing is uploaded, which is also why they do not appear on your other devices.

Planning a dark night

Light pollution and sky brightness are both selectable as a layer under the Conditions group. They are not the same measurement: light pollution is what the satellite sees looking down, which is a good proxy for what to point a camera away from, while sky brightness is modeled looking up and is given as a Bortle class. Turn them on to pick the ground before you plan the night, then fade them back so you can see the roads under them.

Offline Access

Offline download

Saves the area you are looking at as a named region, recording the bounding box, a zoom range and the basemap that was showing.

Map regions

The regions you have marked. Each one shows the ground it covers, the tile count at your chosen zoom range, and roughly how much space it will take.

Download for offline

Fetches the tiles and keeps them on the device. It stores the selected basemap plus any layers switched on over it, and the panel names everything it is about to store before you press it.

What can be downloaded

Byways Topo, USGS Topo and Imagery, the state aerial and lidar layers, BLM, and the FAA ceilings.

Save as a picture

A photograph of the map as it looks right now, saved to your device. It is not the same as a downloaded region: a picture cannot be panned or zoomed, but it needs no account, no network and almost no space.

Sharing

Sending someone the view

The share button in the top menu bar copies a link to exactly what you are looking at: the maps you have open, the basemap under them, and the view itself.

Sending someone a place

A saved waypoint or a dropped pin has its own Share. The link opens the map over that coordinate with the name on screen.

Sending someone a folder

Sharing a folder requires both people to be signed in and synced. If you are not signed in, Share exports the folder as a GPX file instead.

Working on a folder together

Invite someone to a folder as a viewer or an editor. An editor can add, change and remove waypoints, and both of you keep your work. Waypoints merge one at a time rather than the newer save taking the whole folder, so your new pin survives while they rename a different one. If you both change the same waypoint, the later change stands.

The folder stays yours. An invitee cannot delete it, refile it into their own folders, or pass it on to anyone else. Withdraw the invitation at any time and it leaves their device on their next sync.

A trip is a folder with dates on it, so all of this applies to trips as well, and the dates travel between your own devices along with everything else.

Layers

Basemaps and overlays

One basemap at a time — it is the ground everything else is drawn on. Overlays stack over it in any number, each with its own switch and its own opacity, so public land, forest roads, weather and airspace can be turned up or faded back without disturbing the map underneath.

Not responding

A red badge means that layer’s service is not answering. Some are known to be intermittent.

The drone layers

Nothing here is a flight authorization. It is the FAA’s own data drawn on a different map, and it can be out of date.

Drone ceilings

The UAS Facility Map: the grid LAANC reads when it approves a flight. The number is how high LAANC will grant that authorization instantly — not a speed limit for the sky. Every cell on the grid is inside controlled airspace, and a Part 107 flight there needs authorization whatever the cell says. A ceiling of 0 does not mean the airspace is closed; it means nothing is pre-approved and the request goes to further coordination or FAA DroneZone instead.

Outside controlled airspace there is no grid at all. That is Class G, where no authorization is needed — an absent cell is an answer, not a gap.

Restrictions and advisories

Red is do-not-fly: airspace where flight is prohibited or restricted.

Amber asks for care rather than permission, and it covers two different things. Over wilderness and Park Service land, flying across is not itself prohibited — taking off, landing and operating from the ground are. It means find somewhere else to stand, not somewhere else to fly. Alert and warning areas restrict nothing at all; they mark where other aircraft are doing something unusual.

Same-day temporary flight restrictions, such as wildfires and VIP movements, are NOTAMs and are not carried here. Check the FAA’s current advisory list before you fly — the card links to it.

Military operating areas are left out on purpose. They do not restrict civilian flight, and their floors generally sit well above where a drone flies.

Trip planner

What the trip planner does

Give it your stops and it works out the distances between them, how many days that adds up to, and where each day breaks. It also draws the actual road route.

Turn-by-turn directions

Not today. Halfstop is built for planning and photography rather than navigation, and turn-by-turn would mean metered routing services that push the subscription price up.

Accounts

Do I need one?

Not for browsing, planning or drawing on the map. An account is needed to save your work and to sync it between devices.

Sign in and your folders follow you. Sign out and they are cleared from that device, because they live on your account and come back the moment you sign in again. If the last sync did not go through, they stay put instead, and the panel says so.

What syncing carries

Folders and the pins inside them.

Photographs stay on the device they were added to — only the reference travels, so a picture taken on your phone will not appear on your laptop.

Exports

GPX by default, which is what Garmin, Gaia, AllTrails and every handheld will import. GeoJSON is offered alongside it for anything that wants the full properties.

Subscriptions

Canceling a subscription

Whenever you like, without asking anybody.

If you subscribed here, open Manage subscription on your account page. It is also under Plan in the settings menu, the gear at the top of any page including this one. Either one opens the payment provider’s own pages, where you can also switch between monthly and yearly or change the card.

If you subscribed through the App Store, Apple handles it instead: Settings, your name, Subscriptions. Only Apple can end an App Store subscription.

Canceling stops the next payment rather than cutting you off. Premium runs to the end of the period you have already paid for, then the account goes back to being a free one.

Nothing of yours is deleted. Folders, waypoints and notes all stay, and export keeps working, because getting your own data out is free and always will be. What stops is the metered part: folders no longer travel between devices, and the layers and downloads that cost money each time become unavailable again. Subscribe again and it all resumes.

Closing the account is a different thing and does delete everything. Cancel the subscription first, or you will carry on paying for an account that is not there.

Closing your account

This closes the account and removes your folders and pins from the server. Signing in again will not bring any of it back. What is saved on this device is left alone, and export works right up until you press it, so take a GPX first if you want one.

If you pay for Premium, cancel the subscription before closing the account. Closing it does not stop a subscription bought through the App Store. Only Apple can do that.

It is here rather than on the account page on purpose. It is the one thing in this app that cannot be undone, and a button that ends an account does not belong next to the one that signs you out of it.

Getting help

Opening a support inquiry

Write to support@halfstop.app. That is the whole process. There is no form to fill in and no account needed to send one — if the thing that is wrong is that you cannot sign in, you can still write.

What makes an answer quicker: which page or screen you were on, what you expected, and what happened instead. A screenshot usually says it faster than a paragraph. If it is something on the map, roughly where — a place name or a coordinate — and which layers were switched on, because a lot of what looks like a map problem is one layer not having data over that ground.

Mail lands in a queue that one person reads, so a reply is not instant. Everything sent to that address is read.

The same address handles the things the terms and the privacy notice point at it for: a copy of your data, a correction, or a deletion you would rather not do with the button above.
