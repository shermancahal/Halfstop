Roadmap

Plan the light before you drive to it.

A map for photographers. Drop a pin anywhere and Halfstop works out when the light arrives and which way to face — golden hour, blue hour, moonrise, the Milky Way's core, tonight's aurora chance, the next lunar eclipse, and whether the valley will fill with fog by dawn. Underneath it: federal, state and topographic mapping you can download before you leave signal.

Open the map What it does

What it does

The light, at the pin, on the night

The whole day drawn as a bar: night, twilight, golden hour, daylight and back. Beside it the moon, with how much of it is lit and when it clears the horizon; the galactic core, with how high it gets and which nights it is up in true darkness; tonight's aurora chance from NOAA; the next lunar eclipse with its contact times; and the odds of fog, worked out from the forecast's own ingredients.

Which way to face

Sun, moon and Milky Way drawn as lines from the pin, across the actual ground. Pull the time slider and they swing, so you can see whether the core clears the ridge and where the sun will break at 06:20 — before you commit to a two-hour drive and a dark walk in.

Federal and state mapping, stacked

Public land and who manages it, Forest Service roads with surface and season, BLM routes, USGS topo and imagery, light pollution and Bortle sky brightness, FAA drone ceilings and flight restrictions, radar, cloud cover and the direction a warned storm is moving — plus state agency layers for thirty states. Stack any number of them and fade each one independently.

The spots you keep

Save a location with a colour and an icon, pin photographs to it so next year you remember what the light was doing, and sort everything into folders you name. Import a GPX, KML, KMZ or GeoJSON straight off your computer — read in the browser, never uploaded — and export any folder back out as GPX. Sign in and your folders follow you to the next device.

A map drawn to be read, not admired

Byways Topo is our own style rather than somebody else's pixels: an atlas palette borrowed from Rand McNally and National Geographic, terrain you can actually read relief off, unpaved roads drawn louder than the paved ones, and route shields and park symbols rendered here so they cannot go missing. USGS, Esri and OpenStreetMap basemaps sit alongside it, one press away.

It works where the signal does not

Mark the ground you care about, see honestly what it will cost in tiles and megabytes, and take it with you; or save the view as a picture, which costs nothing and is often the whole answer on a phone in a canyon. Every sun, moon and Milky Way figure is arithmetic done on the device, so the panel that decides your evening does not need a bar of signal.

Where this is going

What is shipped, what is next, and what is further out.

  • Shipped Sun, moon, Milky Way, aurora, eclipse and fogOne panel, computed on the device for the pin you selected: light phases as a bar, moon illumination and rise, the galactic core's window, NOAA aurora chance, lunar eclipse contact times, and fog likelihood with the reason attached.
  • Shipped The lines on the mapSun, moon and galactic core drawn as bearings from the pin, moved by a time slider, so a plan is checked against the ridge in front of you rather than against a table of numbers.
  • Shipped Federal and state layersPublic land, Forest Service and BLM roads, USGS topo and imagery, drone ceilings and restrictions, light pollution, weather and storm motion — and state agency layers for thirty states, each one checked against the live service before it was wired in.
  • Shipped Waypoints, folders and photosSave and style a spot, attach photographs to it, organise everything into folders, import and export GPX, KML, KMZ and GeoJSON, and keep folders in step across devices once you sign in.
  • Shipped Offline regions and trip planningDownload the ground you will be standing on, and plan the drive around it — how long a queue of stops really takes once eating, fuel and sleep are counted, and what is worth a detour within a few miles of the way.
  • Next An app in the two storesThe site already installs to a home screen and opens with no network. Next is the native shell around the same code, which is also what unlocks proper offline storage for the basemaps that reserve it for native toolkits.
  • Next More states, more agenciesThirty states have layers wired in. The rest have been surveyed and the good sources are known; each one is a matter of probing the service and confirming its fields say something useful.
  • Later Solar eclipses and drawn routesPaths of totality alongside the lunar eclipses already here, and sketching a route in the browser to export as GPX for whatever you actually navigate with.
